<p style="text-align: justify;">
“Jewish Writing in the Global South: Exploring Contiguities of Indian Jewish Writing with Maghrebi Jewish and Subaltern Indian Writing” (JWIGS) is the first research project to explore Indian Jewish writing in relation to its various contiguities in different socio-historical and cultural contexts. It develops an innovative perspective on Indian Jewish writing with the aim of positioning it in relation to other subaltern Indian literatures and Maghrebi Jewish writing as another manifestation of Jewish diasporic writing in the Global South. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
JWIGS offers a fruitful interdisciplinary intervention in studies in Jewish literature and postcolonial studies as well as South Asian literary studies and Maghrebi literatures. These are usually distinct fields of inquiry which are rarely brought together. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The main questions JWIGS seeks to elucidate are: 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
•	How is Indian Jewish writing positioned vis-à-vis its colonial and postcolonial contexts in India in comparison to other subalterns in India, and what are the consequences of such intersectionalities (Jewish, Dalit, female)? 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
•	How are postcolonial and diasporic identities reframed by the Indian and Maghrebin Jewish communities? 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
•	How are dominant Eurocentric narratives of Jewish trauma and belonging challenged by such a reframing? 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
•	How is the theorization of the Jewish literary complex impacted by their positionalities? 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
JWIGS is comprised of two complementary sub-projects which cumulatively serve to address the topic systematically and comprehensively: 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
“Corporeal Inscriptions of Histories of Trauma, Identity, and Resistance: Jewish and Dalit Writing in India” elaborates contiguities of Indian Jewish writing specific to the Indian setting. Comparatively examining Dalit and Jewish semi-autobiographical narratives, it demonstrates that subaltern histories are inscribed not primarily through institutional records or formal historiography. It rather contends, by situating the body as the locus of memory and identity, that subaltern histories are inscribed more viscerally than institutionally, emphasizing the material, affective, and performative dimensions of corporeal marginality, and proposes a comparative framework wherein the corporeal lens offers critical insight into the ways trauma, memory, and subalternity are intertwined in literary production across disparate historical and geographical contexts. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
“Cultural Hybridities and Jewish Memory: A Comparative Analysis of Indian and Maghrebi Jewish Literatures” explores in a comparative approach migration narratives and cultural memory in Indian and Maghrebin Jewish writing, focusing on how these literary texts represent displacement, trauma, identity, and resilience in postcolonial contexts. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Altogether, JWIGS aims to illuminate the literary voices of Jewish communities from South Asia and North Africa, which are groups traditionally marginalized in Eurocentric Jewish studies. Their experiences are shaped by British and French colonial legacies, and their narratives unfold beyond India and North Africa across ‘doubly’ diasporic spaces in the anglophone world and in France as well as in Israel. The cross-pollination achieved with this approach allows a comprehensive exploration of Indian Jewish literature as it has never been attempted before and offers the theoretical and methodological basis also for venturing into other clusters of the Jewish literary complex. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
By positioning Indian Jewish writing through the exploration of its contiguities within the Jewish literary complex as well as vis-à-vis adjoining literary complexes of different provenance, JWIGS offers a significant contribution to the study of Jewish literatures, to the study of postcolonial literatures, and to the study of subaltern literatures. 
</p>
