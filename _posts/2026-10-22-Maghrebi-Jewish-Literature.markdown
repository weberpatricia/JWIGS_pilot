<p style="text-align: justify;">
Secular Maghrebi Jewish writing offers a rich perspective on Jewish communities in North Africa. From the nineteenth century onwards, it was shaped by French colonialism and, after the independence of the former colonies and protectorates, frequently by migration. Writing in this literary context becomes an act of remembrance and historical recovery, bringing to light experiences and narratives that have often remained at the margins of dominant accounts of Jewish modernity, diaspora, and identity, which have primarily centred on European and American Jewish experiences. Much of Maghrebi Jewish literature was written in French, the language of colonial education, and is often studied under the name ‘Francophone Maghrebi Jewish Literature.’ Yet its pages carry the traces of the languages spoken at home, from Judeo-Arabic to Judeo-Berber and Haketia.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Each North African country followed a distinct colonial and historical trajectory, which was reflected in literary representations of Jewish experiences, cultural affiliations, and identities in different ways. The French occupation of Algeria lasted from 1830–1962, and France established protectorates in both Tunisia and Morocco, from 1881 and 1912, respectively, to 1956. While France exercised indirect rule over Tunisia and Morocco, the 1870 Crémieux Decree, which granted French citizenship to Algerian Jews, marked a pivotal moment in their history within this distinct colonial context. The granting, revocation, and restoration of citizenship in 1870, 1940, and 1943, respectively, would later find expression in literary representations of Jewish life in North Africa.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Maghrebi Jewish literary texts highlight the heterogeneous and multifaceted nature of Jewish identities in North Africa, reflecting the diverse cultural affiliations and historical experiences that shaped them, including Arab, Amazigh (Berber), French, and Jewish heritage. Many Maghrebi Jewish writers have written in French, including Albert Memmi, Jacques Derrida, and Hélène Cixous, whose works engage with questions of identity, language, cultural belonging, and the legacies of colonialism. Their writing reflects the linguistic and cultural worlds inherited from previous generations, engaging with languages and traditions such as Judeo-Arabic, Judeo-Berber, Judeo-Haketia as well as other linguistic influences shaped by distinct historical and geographical contexts. For example, Jacques Derrida’s <i>Le monolinguisme de l'autre (Monolingualism of the Other)</i> (1998) examines his relationship to the French language through his experience of dispossession as an Algerian Jew stripped of his French citizenship under the Vichy regime. These overlapping cultural and linguistic inheritances reveal the diversity of experiences that inform literary representations of Jewish identity in North Africa.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Maghrebi Jewish literature provides a valuable framework for examining how these experiences were represented, negotiated, and reinterpreted in literary texts. Across a diverse body of literary works, themes such as colonialism, citizenship, relations with Muslim communities, trauma, language, identity, diaspora, and belonging emerge as important themes through which to explore the diverse and historically situated experiences that shaped Jewish identities across the Maghreb.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Francophone secular Maghrebi Jewish literature is generally considered to have emerged in 1896 with the publication of <i>Rabbin: roman de mœurs juives (The Rabbi: A Novel of Jewish Customs)</i> by the Algerian Jewish poet Sadia Lévy and the French writer Robert Randau. Women writers were among the earliest and most noteworthy figures of Maghrebi Jewish literature:  Elissa Rhaïs became the first North African author published in France with her novel <i>Saâda la marocaine (Saâda the Moroccan)</i> in 1919. Blanche Bendahan received the Prix de l’Académie Française for her novel <i>Mazaltob (Good Fortune)</i> in 1930, and Berthe Bénichou-Aboulkers play <i>La Kahéna, Reine Berbère (The Kahina, Berber Queen)</i> was the first book by a woman to be published in North Africa, in Algiers in 1933.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The next generations carried Maghrebi Jewish literature through the upheavals of the mid-twentieth century. Writers like Albert Memmi, Gisèle Halimi, and Hélène Cixous transformed the memory of a lost Maghrebi world into memoir and autobiographical fiction, literary forms which have dominated Maghrebi Jewish literature since the 1970s and 1980s.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
This tradition continues to evolve as contemporary authors extend its reach across film, theatre, stand-up comedy, and comic books, from the screenplays of Eliette Abécassis and Nine Moati to the graphic novels of Joann Sfar, demonstrating the vitality and adaptability of Maghrebi Jewish literary production.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Maghrebi Jewish literature invites us to consider identities lived in the plural, shaped at once by Arab, Amazigh, French, and Jewish inheritances, and held together by the languages and memories passed down across generations. It is a multilingual literature, written for the most part in French, that carries within its sentences the traces of the languages spoken at home, from Judeo-Arabic and Judeo-Berber to Haketia and Judeo-Italian, inserting the linguistic and cultural heritage of earlier generations into their Francophone writing. It asks us to look at what it meant to live under colonialism, between citizenship and dispossession, and to see how Jews and Muslims shared streets, homes, and daily routines across the Maghreb. Indeed, it invites us to approach the Maghreb as a shared cultural space and to read literary voices that preserve, in their language, the memory of a world that has passed.
</p>
