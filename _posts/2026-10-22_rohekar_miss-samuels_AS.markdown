<p style="text-align: justify;">
Sheela Rohekar, born in 1942, is a Bene Israel who writes in Hindi. In her earlier novels, Dinat (1978) and Taviz (2005), as well as in her latest novel Pallipaar (2022), she does not engage with her Jewish heritage. Miss Samuel: A Jewish-Indian Saga (2013) was her response to the question of why she had never done so; it was, as she acknowledged in an interview, a question that “kept troubling [her]” (Aafreedi 2013). As a possible reason for avoiding Jewish themes, she offered her “apprehension that nobody would be able to understand the concerns and sensibilities of a community as small as [hers] in this huge sea of humanity”; and yet, Miss Samuel won the Uttar Pradesh Hindi Sansthan’s Premchand Award (Aafreedi 2013). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Based in Delhi and Lucknow, Rohekar is married to the Hindu writer Ravindra Varma (Wessler 2018: 187). Marrying out is reflected by Rohekar not so much in relation to the Bene Israel community but rather from the perspective of becoming the outsider in another community: “I felt scared after the marriage. I was well aware how I would be chastised by society. And that is exactly what happened. The elderly lesser educated women in my husband’s extended family accepted me reluctantly but refused to accept even water or food from my hands.” (Aafreedi 2013) Intermarriage is a prominent theme in Rohekar’s fiction; in Taviz (The Amulet), she explores a Hindu–Muslim marriage, and it recurs, if embedded in a more diffuse narrative, also in her Miss Samuel.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Rohekar’s has the widest scope of the Indian Jewish novels discussed here. Through the memories of her main character, Seema Samuel, who has retired to an old age home near Pune, she unfolds in a frequently fragmented and shifting narrative the story of a Bene Israel family across six generations. Ranging from the shores of Konkan, where the Bene Israel first settled after their mythical shipwreck, to Ahmedabad (Amdavad) in Gujarat, their story begins with Isaji Eloji (Isaac Elijah) marrying a Hindu woman. The perceived transgression, which entails the alleged “blackening” of the purity of the Bene Israel, continues to haunt their descendants. As collaborators in the British colonial project, they experience the uncertainty of independence; against the promise of Israel, later generations of the family are made to feel outsiders in India and fall victim to communal violence. Seema’s introspective reflections on the tensions between Jewishness and Indianness, persecution and the yearning for acceptance, are enriched with her interactions with the other aged women of different backgrounds in her old age home as she slowly declines.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Though first published in Hindi in the same year as Silliman’s The Man with Many Hats, the trajectory of Rohekar’s Miss Samuel is very different and, ultimately, untypical of the bulk of Indian Jewish writing. The novel challenges many of the constants that emerged in anglophone Indian Jewish writing in the wake of the translation of Mahadevan’s Shulamith. While the same themes are negotiated, Rohekar’s novel projects fragmentation, dissolution, and perspectivation. The Bene Israel community is never shown with clear contours from the inside, but is mostly characterized through its timid and helpless external relations. Distinctly situated within the historical setting in which Seema commits her memories to paper, the novel includes topical references to the aftermath of the Second Iraq War and to the Second Lebanon War as well as various outbreaks of communal violence in India. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The supposed permeation of Miss Samuel with an essential Jewishness is implicitly reclaimed for the novel by its translator, Madhu Singh, when she muses in her foreword if “a non-Jewish translator [can] translate a writer’s Jewishness?” Singh posits an “implicit subtext that renders a book Jewish, besides the explicit themes” and wonders what scope to “adjust, adapt or compromise” the non-Jewish translator has to translate “a sensibility and ethos different from her own.” (MSxx) Problematic as such a potentially essentialist statement is, Singh seems to respond to the frequently reiterated assertion in the novel that the Bene Israel and their customs are unknown and unrecognizable to the majority in India and that their position within the diversity of the nation is therefore destabilized and uncertain. The contiguities Singh evokes are consequently limited exclusively to the Indian context; the differences in the experiences between Indian Jews and Ashkenazi, Sephardic, or Mizrachi Jews escape the translator. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Israel, so prominent in Mahadevan’s novel and a potential alternative in Silliman’s, remains the unfulfilled dream of Seema’s father Samuel, a vague idea which cannot provide any real hope. Later in the novel, it is rather the perceived destructive potential of Israel, echoing Shulamith’s apprehensions, which comes to the fore. The only mention of the Holocaust in Miss Samuel emerges indirectly as a referent for Jewish existence, and does so with the geographically erroneous location of Auschwitz in Germany (MS241). The Holocaust is evoked in relation to Israel as a moral imperative. Against the background of the Second Lebanon War from July to August 2006, Ameena, a schoolfriend of Seema’s, and a Muslim who lost all her family in the communal violence of 2002, maintains that Auschwitz “has been preserved as a symbol of racism, intolerance, vandalism and genocide because it was a witness to the most heinous and torturous murder of sixty lakh Jews during the Second World War.” (MS241) Ameena invokes the commemorative function of Auschwitz in the face of the “devastation” wrought by Israel (MS241) and, chastising Seema for being Jewish, insists on the analogous victimization of Muslims in what seems almost an anticipation of Michael Rothberg’s (2009) concept of multidirectional memory: “look at me and say, isn’t the killing of the innocent people of Iraq cruel? Shouldn’t Lebanese cities like Tyre and Jbeil also have similar concentration camps as symbols of racism and barbarism?” (MS242) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Miss Samuels’s focus is otherwise exclusively on Jewish life in India through Seema’s reminiscences. Like Silliman, Rohekar, too, unfolds historical background information, in her case focalized through Seema who reads and re-reads an essay about the Bene Israel entitled “The Rise of a Forgotten Race” (MS192) that was written by her brother Bobby (Mikhail Samuel).
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The essay sketches the history of a minuscule and marginalized community always afraid of being caught in the lethal confrontation between more assertive communities and, in times of crises, withdrawing into itself—with the result that “over time, they became narrow-minded, dull, timid, fatalist and insignificant and stood on the edge of loss of memory.” (MS214) Following the transition from rural to urban environments, the Bene Israel seek to emerge from their anonymity and aspire to create an identity. Yet their alignment with British colonial rule leads to allegations of misplaced loyalty and, once again, to their defensive withdrawal. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Though the term “antisemitism” does not occur in Rohekar’s novel, Bobby’s essay intriguingly includes a reference to the blood libel (MS204). However, the anticipated persecution provoked by the reconfigured blood libel mentioned in the essay—supposedly perpetrated upon Muslims in Iraq and historically situated in close proximity to the notorious blood libel of Tiszaeszlár of 1882, which stoked European antisemitism—remains a mere potentiality (MS204). The blood libel is not actualized in India; nor do the Bene Israel suffer collateral damage in an earlier riot between Parsis and Muslims in India. But, and this is the momentous conclusion from these narrow escapes: “They knew the shadow of fear.” (MS204)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
And yet, reiterating the trope of the absence of any persecution in India, which was invoked also by Mahadevan and Silliman, the essay contrasts this constant uncertainty with the ultimately ambivalent guarantees offered by India:
</p>

> Generous and magnanimous, India sheltered them like a huge, shady tree. […] India never sent away or tortured the Jews. They were never insulted for being “Semitic” and got all the rights, like all citizens, from the Constitution.
> 
> Even so, in the atmosphere in which they lived and grew up, they were seen as outsiders. They never experienced the joy of being included. They were not humiliated, but the circles of human empathy and concern hardly touched them. (MS214–15)


<p style="text-align: justify;">
According to the essay, the Bene Israel therefore “always felt uprooted,” they felt “incomplete, weary and sad,” without prospects and, once again, withdrew—in this instance into a religious fantasy:
</p>

> They never felt the courage, concern or excitement enough to contribute to the Indian milieu, politics or culture. Perhaps because of this, many generations went to their graves, hoping to return to the soil from whence they had come. (MS215)


<p style="text-align: justify;">
The transition to the yearning for the Promised Land steers the essay to the negotiation of Indian Jewish identities between India and Israel, which is prominent also in Mahadevan’s and Silliman’s novels. “Today”—Bobby’s essay is supposedly published in 1972—
</p>

> many of the Bene Israel settled in India are about to leave for Israel, in the hope of change, security and miracles for the success of the future generations. Even then, there are hundreds, like the writer of this piece, who honestly believe that only this country, India, […] is their motherland. After being with her for centuries, their identity is bound to this country.
> 
> For such people, India is their Israel. (MS215–16)


<p style="text-align: justify;">
This affirmation of the Bene Israel commitment to India, which once again echoes the other novels discussed in this chapter, is, however, suffused with ironic ambivalence in Miss Samuel—before he can see his essay in print, Bobby dies at the hands of a mob during communal riots (MS70, 72–3). Though he was not targeted as a Jew, but mistaken for a Muslim, Bobby’s violent death nevertheless contests his commitment to India by finally actualizing “the shadow of fear” long since instilled into the Bene Israel—the novel is intensely aware of the hermeneutical challenge this presents to the essay: “So, does it tell a completely different story?” (MS70)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
What eventually leads to the actualization of their victimhood is that the Bene Israel are an unknown entity within Indian majority culture. They are conflated with Christians, or, even more fatally, with Muslims. The latter identification is suggested by their circumcision, sometimes forcefully scrutinized and considered sufficient evidence for brutal murder. While Bobby’s Muslim friend escapes the mob violence by pretending to be a Hindu, Bobby’s insistence on his Jewish identity cannot save him because of the obscurity of the Bene Israel (MS129). Challenging the certainties of Mahadevan (S57), Bobby’s mother, as she descends into insanity, keeps asking: “but my Bobby was Israyel, wasn’t he?” (MS73) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Within the structure of the novel, the essay—fragmented into several passages as it is read by Seema toward the end of the novel—serves as a foil against which the family saga is retrospectively contextualized, or even as a blueprint for the novel whose relevance emerges in hindsight; but it exemplifies also a collective trajectory which offers Seema a paradigm in relation to which she seeks to define herself, if with some doubts and misgivings about its potentially prescriptive nature:
</p>

> I wonder who I am searching for.
> 
> My long-passed forefathers? Bobby? My roots, or myself?
> 
> Am I subsumed in them? Does my own identity open up layer by layer like Bobby’s essay? Are our courage, fear, frustrations, loneliness, attacks and counter attacks, selfishness, thinking and helplessness a copy of the culture and status of a race or community? (MS205)


<p style="text-align: justify;">
Seema’s doubts are, moreover, directed in a metafictional observation at the inevitable failure of words to represent reality adequately: “The writer believed the essay was complete, but so many things remain unsaid; did he ever know? Who knows how many sounds of lost and forgotten stories there are in the leftover blank spaces around the words.” (MS216) Rohekar’s novel, it is to be assumed, is an attempt to fill some of the blank spaces. Yet hers, too, is a doomed effort: “Words in themselves are never complete because they have neither beginnings nor endings.” (MS216)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The self-reflexivity of Miss Samuel distinguishes it from both Mahadevan’s and Silliman’s novels, neither of whom seeks to complicate their narratives. It seems to be rooted in the pessimism which permeates Rohekar’s novel. Referring to different forms of discrimination and communal violence, the author reflects that “stories never end. Each story gives birth to another story, just as a cell divides into two, four and then into multiple cells”; Rohekar recognizes the malignant potential of this proliferation in analogy to cancerous growth: “Sometimes, a story and a cell may become distorted. When such a cell multiplies, it becomes a malignant tumour.” (MS79) If not explicitly, this seems to align the destructive force of narrative distortion which perpetuates division with the malicious actions it fuels and is fuelled by: 
</p>

> Every present may not dive deep into the past and create history. Sometimes, the present burns and crackles on the edge of a crater, like volcanic lava, and, instead of sinking into its depths, asks several questions from life, holds it responsible. (MS80)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
By extrapolation, a strong moral imperative adheres then to the telling of stories.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The Bene Israel community’s, and Seema’s, experience is impacted also by that of their surroundings. While the violence of Partition finds no representation in the novel, the impending struggle for independence and its implications for the family is reflected in her grandfather David Reuben’s bitter response to attempts to recruit him for the independence movement. Being a jailor for the British, he is apprehensive and offers a bland assessment of the situation of the Jews which connects across the novel with Bobby’s essay:
</p>

> Did you ever, at any point in time, think about us? We have lived with you for thousands of years, but did anyone ever extend a helping hand, even a faint consolation or a hint of concern? Did anyone ever want to know about our joys and sorrows, our festivals, religion or customs?
> 
> We were pushed into the slot of “mlechchha” or “pardesi” [i.e., outsider, stranger]. Did we ever have meals together or become a part of your prayers and worship or marriage ceremonies? Tell me, have you ever experienced the uncertainties of the future, the fear that minorities face, their alienation and isolation from the rest of the people? Or have you felt ashamed of your own timidity, wariness, opportunism or self-centredness, like us? Have you ever dreamt of having a home, higher education, a decent job, and sharing your joys and sorrows with society for generations? Tell me!
> 
> If we stand with you, will you give us all those things we have struggled to achieve since the last two thousand years? Has it ever occurred to you that once the power is in your hands, you will also use us like the British? (MS119)


<p style="text-align: justify;">
The discrimination experienced by Rohekar’s Jewish characters at the hands of the Hindu majority manifests itself after independence for instance in Bobby’s failure to find a job—“Even if you are Indian, you do not belong here” (MS128)—but it surfaces also in the obstacles with which Samuel’s search for accommodation is met. When he is finally successful, it turns out that the flat is located on a segregated floor reserved for outsiders. The flat is eventually lost due to the machinations of the Hindu family into which Seema’s brother Daniel marries. This mixed marriage is not shown from the inside in relation to Bene Israel restrictions but as an exploitative act from the outside which eventually dispossesses Seema as the sole survivor of her generation. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Seema’s niece Mili (Milcah) also marries out into a Hindu family. Again, no injunctions from within the Bene Israel community are mentioned, but the toll her desertion exacts from Mili is debilitating and seems to reflect the writer’s own experience: “I couldn’t save anything of my older self.” (MS254) The resulting reconfiguration of her identity strikes Mili in her core: “Even if there was no compulsion to follow or not to follow caste or religion, the vulgar display of your identity being altered is so disturbing.” (MS255)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
In the end, the pessimism that pervades Rohekar’s novel about the Bene Israel in India controverts any nostalgia about their history. Indeed, nostalgia is denounced in Miss Samuel as poisonous:
 </p>

> Memory plays strange games. The flow of nostalgia doesn’t follow a set pattern. Hanging on to the edges of some special places, colours, circumstances, people, time periods or snatches of sentences, it plunges into another time and spreads over the present, like a poisonous vine. The making and the unmaking of the collages of memory cannot be checked by the past. (MS99–100)
