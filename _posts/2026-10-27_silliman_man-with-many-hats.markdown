<p style="text-align: justify;">
Jael Silliman was born into the Baghdadi community of Calcutta (Kolkata) in 1955. After long periods of living in the US, she now lives in Calcutta. Holding degrees from various American universities, including a doctorate in education from Columbia University, she became an Associate Professor of Women Studies at the University of Iowa before working for the Human Rights Unit of the Ford Foundation. Silliman has a number of scholarly publications on transnational feminist issues, women’s rights, and reproductive rights to her credit and initiated a digital archive of the rich cultural heritage of the Jewish community in Calcutta (Silliman 2019). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<em>The Man with Many Hats</em> is Silliman’s first novel which is clearly informed not only by autobiographical experiences but also by the author’s scholarly interests. Like Mahadevan’s <em>Shulamith</em>, it focuses on the vagaries experienced by an Indian Jewish family. In her case, however, dominated by the flamboyant and mercurial character of Morris Selman, the family is of Baghdadi origin and the narrative is mostly set in Calcutta. When Morris’s extramarital escapades become unbearable, his wife Sarah separates from him and eventually emigrates with her son Jacob to Israel. In the meantime, as the Baghdadi community in Calcutta dwindles, Sarah’s elderly and traditional mother leaves for London. Yet the novel’s main character is Sarah’s daughter Rachel whose own liberation from her father leads her to university in America, briefly back to India, and eventually to her marriage to Ronen, a Hindu fellow academic in the US. When Ronen, too, becomes unfaithful and their two daughters are old enough to fend for themselves, they divorce and Rachel decides to return to India for good, where she is joined by Sarah who leaves Israel.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The eccentric plurality of head coverings invoked in the novel’s title is both symbolic and programmatic. Morris, the man with many hats, may not be a flawless character, but at least later in life, he demonstrates more penetration than other characters and the diversity of his hats is paradigmatic of the cosmopolitanism which pervades the novel. From its very beginning—it is initially set in the 1960s, only a few years after Mahadevan’s—Silliman emphasizes in a chronotopic characterization the cosmopolitan spirit of Calcutta, at the heart of which she situates the Baghdadi community: 
</p>

> At that point in time, the early sixties, there were still fairly substantial communities of Jewish, Armenian, Anglo-Indian and Chinese families in Calcutta. In any case, as the ex-commercial hub of India and because of its proximity to Burma, Calcutta’s population was very much a crossroads of peoples from all parts of India as well as from other parts of the neighbouring world. There was also a shifting population of Europeans, British and Americans visiting or living in Calcutta for pleasure or business. (MMH6)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Illustrative passages like this serve Silliman not only to position the Baghdadi community from a historical perspective within Indian society but also to illuminate its character and that of the novel’s main figures who, in turn, exemplify the Baghdadis of Calcutta:
</p>

> Morris was proud of his Jewish heritage and had an abiding respect for its traditions and values. Though there never were many Jews in Calcutta, Morris had grown up as part of this community that had played a vital role in the city’s development. The Calcutta Jews had come to the city from Iraq and Syria when the English first came to trade in India, and had grown and had prospered under the Raj. (MMH10)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Against this background of communal affiliation and cosmopolitan integration, the Baghdadi Jews are described by Silliman as collaborators in the British colonial project:
</p>

> Jews served as middlemen between the British and the local people and were ideal in this role. We spoke English and wore Western clothes, but we were also accepted and comfortable in India. Unlike the British, India was our home. (MMH43)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Yet their status in India changes significantly with independence: “Favoured by the British and commercially successful, many were unsure of their economic futures when India gained Independence.” (MMH10) Even as it chronicles the subsequent depletion of the Baghdadi community, Silliman’s novel reiterates the commitment to India already articulated in Mahadevan’s <em>Shulamith</em>. Morris “was optimistic about India’s future and wanted to be part of the new, emerging India”; he “resolved to be both Jewish and Indian, and was quite sure he could fuse the two identities successfully.” (MMH11)

<em>The Man with Many Hats</em> articulates an Indian Jewish exceptionalism similar to Mahadevan’s, if slightly reconfigured. The distinct difference of the Baghdadi Jewish experience in contrast to the predominant Ashkenazi paradigm is elaborated by Silliman as she explains the provenance of Morris’s surname, Selman, which is shared by her own. Morris invites some Jewish American tourists into his Calcutta home who bear the same surname and are very keen on genealogical research. The Jewish American Selmans seek to claim their familial and, implicitly also their experiential, affinity (MMH42) but are cautioned that the Calcutta, or Baghdadi, Jews are Arab Jews whose experience is categorically different from the Ashkenazi paradigm. (MMH41) Other than Mahadevan, Silliman describes Indian Jewish exceptionalism without evoking the Holocaust, which remains an absence in her novel. Like Mahadevan, however, Silliman insists that the Baghdadis “never faced any anti-Semitism.” (MMH43)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
In contrast to the Bene Israel experience, Silliman emphasizes the anglicization of the Baghdadis, which originates in their commitment to trade and the colonial project. (MMH44) Exemplified with the Baghdadi Selmans, it extends into the post-colonial period through the cultural practices they adopt and the affinities they enjoy; they “read exclusively in English,” go “to see only British and American movies,” and live “at a certain level removed from the majority.” (MMH6) Their insularity is further emphasized with their limited use of Hindi as a language strictly reserved for hierarchical encounters. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The anglicization of the Baghdadi Jews is described as very different from other westernizing influences around them. Cherishing the cosmopolitanism she experiences in her Christian convent school, Rachel reflects that “being Jewish meant that she was a little more different than the rest of her close friends,” who are also “westernized,” but whose “lives were nonetheless bound by deeply rooted Indian customs as well as the Indian festivals that punctuated the calendar year.” (MMH47) Their anglicization moreover allows most of Rachel’s cousins to leave India for “faraway places like England, Canada, Israel or Australia.” (MMH46)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Like Mahadevan’s <em>Shulamith</em>, <em>The Man with Many Hats</em> presents three generations of Jewish women whose response to the strictures of their religion and community values is representative of the changes with which the Indian Jewish community is confronted. While the circumstances of the Bene Israel and Baghdadis are described as rather different in the two novels, the challenges they experience are quite similar. In Silliman’s novel, Grandma Mozelle “firmly believed that her world, and everything in it, should be orderly and consistent with Jewish Halakha.” (MMH51) Eventually, as the Baghdadi community continues to dwindle, she leaves India for England “to join the Jewish community of Calcutta that had relocated there,” because “it’s getting harder and harder to be Jewish in Calcutta. There is practically nobody left. The synagogue feels almost empty, even on High Holidays. It pulls my spirits down.” (MMH78) Religious community and observance determine her decision, which prioritizes these categories over geographical and national affiliations: “She was sad to leave Calcutta, but she looked forward to being part of a thriving Jewish community again.” (MMH78) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The young Sarah in contrast, roughly of the same generation as Shulamith, is described as feeling suffocated by “the extremely self-contained and insular Jewish world she lived in.” (MMH54) Being “bright” and “ambitious,” she “was looking to break out of her more conservative home and the Jewish community where everybody knew everyone else and all their business too.” (MMH53) Her desire for liberation drives her into the marriage to Morris which, eventually, will be no less suffocating for her, and from which she needs to liberate herself as well.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The multicultural framework of Indian society which facilitates, but also endangers, Jewish particularity and monotheism, is perceived by Mozelle as threatening. She never permits the young Sarah to participate in religious festivals of other communities. Rachel, in contrast, is allowed by her mother to play Holi (a Hindu festival celebrating the arrival of spring) with her Hindu friends (MMH71). Sarah is construed by Silliman as the model of an intermediate, more liberal attitude. Insisting that she is “quite different from Granny about this,” she tells Rachel that once she went to college, she took part in every festival she could because she considered this the premise of getting to know the other. (MMH72) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
In the novel, Sarah’s tolerance comes to fruition in Rachel who, like the doctor in <em>Shulamith</em>, eventually marries out. The implicit threat to the continuity of the dwindling community or its potential defilement, which were so prominent in Mahadevan’s novel about the Bene Israel, is not even mentioned in the <em>The Man with Many Hats</em>. Rachel has two daughters from her marriage who, according to the matrilineal principle of the Halakha on which Mozelle insists, would still be considered Jewish. Yet while it is important to her to impart to them a sense of their heritage, this is not so much about being Jewish but, since they were born and live in America, about their Indian origins. She takes them every year on vacation to India because she wants them “to know who they were and where they came from.” (MMH199) Their intense contacts to friends in India “made them part of a reality strikingly different from their own ABCD – American Born Confused Desi – one.” (MMH201)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Earlier generations of Baghdadis, as Sarah notes, “lived happily in the little world that they made for themselves. Up to my Ma’s time they were mostly socialized with one another—living in a small Jewish world that stretched from Baghdad to Shanghai.” (MMH104) For Rachel, her life having been “a sheltered and privileged one” (MMH104), [this] means that she eventually recognizes her own Indianness as deficient. Her feelings reflect the political and historical development of nation-building in the recently independent India into which the Baghdadi community was not fully assimilated and which resulted, as Silliman noted in one of her scholarly publications, in their cultural hybridization (2001: 20). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Having received a scholarship for studying in America—even though “at the Selmans’, where everyone spoke English, wore Western clothes and listened to Western music, she felt she was practically living abroad already!” (MMH90)—Rachel becomes for the first time aware of the distinctively Indian dimension of her identity as she faces the ignorance of her fellow students about India and Indian Jews more specifically. Simultaneously, she recognizes the fissures in her own hybrid identity:
</p>

> In India Rachel felt a Jew among Indians. When she attended her first event at Hillel House she felt an Indian among Jews. Rachel had always grown up knowing she was different from everyone else. In Calcutta difference seemed to be the norm, not the anomaly—every community was distinct and their primary belonging was to their particular communities. Identifying beyond community as an Indian was still a work in progress. There were so many other layers of difference that one encountered in everyday life. People lived in different time periods but shared the same space. (MMH94) 

<p style="text-align: justify;">
The interleaving of time and space projected onto Calcutta turns the city into a multiple chronotope. Throughout Silliman’s novel, lives are reconfigured in relation to competing chronotopes which include next to Calcutta and India also Israel and the US. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The encounter with her fellow students’ ignorance makes Rachel at the same time aware of her own ignorance about India (MMH95). Having experienced her own Indianness, deficient as it may be, her desire goes beyond identification. She wants “to make India a part of her.” (MMH104) The encounter with India outside the sheltered life Rachel used to lead, helps her to position herself in relation to another chronotope: “she was home again—home was no longer the beautiful apartment on Loudon Street, but the whole beautiful country was her home now.” (MMH117) To substantiate this chronotope, Rachel needs to discover the India that is unknown to her, and she goes to work with Adivasis (scheduled tribes), “who were completely marginalized in Indian society.” (MMH117) Her confrontation with the systemically underprivileged reveals that her own Indianness remains deficient and is in further need of (re-)construction. The confrontation with the illusive nature of a comprehensive Indianness, its intrinsic fragmentation and the social breakage invoked with the tribal and caste systems, no less than Rachel’s intrinsic otherness, reasserts that, for her, Indianness signifies cultural hybridization.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Rachel’s identity quest is further complicated by Silliman with the inclusion of Israel, another chronotope, in Rachel’s deliberations. It is the country where Sarah, after her divorce from Morris and fully aware of the sacrifices this move will exact from her, has emigrated together with Rachel’s younger brother, Jacob. Yet for Rachel, Israel—like America—offers no real alternative to India:
</p>

> There was much about Israel that she admired. She liked the idealism and the lifestyle and saw it as an ideal space somewhere between East and West. But India was a powerful force that held her in its grip and captivated her imagination and senses. It was where she felt most at home – where she felt complete. She felt anonymous in the US. (MMH105)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Silliman’s novel is much more reticent about the impact of Israel on Indian Jews than Mahadevan’s, and this may reflect the lesser migration movement to the Jewish State by Baghdadi Jews. While Israel offers a temporary refuge for Sarah after her divorce from Morris and Jacob chooses to settle there, it is rejected by Rachel. Her mother, too, eventually returns to re-join her in Calcutta. Significantly, in contrast to Grandma Mozelle, the anglophone diaspora fails to satisfy Rachel and Sarah. Their attachment to India, or perhaps more specifically Calcutta, is stronger than the pull of any other promised lands.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
In this, Rachel is very different from her husband Ronen, a Hindu from Calcutta, whom she meets and marries in America. When, after decades of marriage, he betrays her and wishes to divorce her, his assimilation to the American way of life is another clear indicator that Rachel remains indebted to her Indian origins. The dialogue between them is indicative of their different identity constructions and the concomitant horizons of expectation:
</p>

> “One in three marriages ends in divorce.”
> 
> “But we’re not Americans,” a bewildered and very weak Rachel responded. Ronen told her to speak for herself. 
> 
> “America is my present and my future. America has made me whom I am. You should be very grateful to this country too, not pining on and on about Calcutta! I find it quite ludicrous. Pathetic, actually, to hear you go on and on about your beloved Cal! For God’s sake grow up Rachel!” (MMH234–44)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Morris, the man of many hats, is the only one to be suspicious of Ronen’s “over-eager embracement of Western ways.” (MMH145) His insight that “nobody could pull off such an abrupt turn around successfully in the long term without sacrificing a great deal of integrity, not to mention self-knowledge” (MMH145), presumably articulates Silliman’s caveats who, like her main character, never broke off her ties to India. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Yet Rachel admires Ronen precisely for his protean abilities, which she herself lacks completely: “Rachel, for her own part, only knew how to be herself. She was not any good at adopting a different persona—even when it would have been in her best interests.” (MMH147) This acknowledgment is intriguing because it signifies an inversion of the antisemitic notion of Jewish identities as protean and threatening. Originating in a European context, the implication is that this calumny leveled at Ashkenazi Jews is irrelevant in the Baghdadi context and offers another affirmation of Indian Jewish exceptionalism, because Jewish identities appear not to be fractured in India. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Silliman nevertheless thematizes the difficulties of positioning Jews within the framework of Indian society, in particular in relation to the caste system. When Ronen informs his parents about his marriage plans, he tells them that Rachel is “a Brahmin” because mentioning her Jewishness would have been “way too foreign sounding.” (MMH135) The Chatterjees are indeed bemused as to what a Jew is. They have some vague idea about the Jews having killed the Christian God, but once this has been explained, they fully accept their daughter-in-law  and demonstrate their tolerance also in the face of institutional intolerance. When Rachel is denied access to a Hindu temple they want to visit, Ma Chatterjee vows never to visit it again in solidarity with Rachel.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Through her good relationship with Ronen’s parents, Rachel comes “even closer to India and to understanding its intimate spaces.” (MMH169) Ronen, in contrast, insists that he has nothing in common with his family. His de-Indianization is emphasized in a minimally focalized observation which concludes the altercation between him and Rachel about their divorce. It emphasizes their different perceptions of themselves in relation to geopolitical affiliations and ultimately offers a variation on the previously discussed notion of the chronotope: “Unlike Rachel, Ronen had found himself in America. He […] had embraced the States as the Promised Land, and had blossomed in a way he had never had a chance to do in India.” (MMH244) The explicit reference to America as a “Promised Land” indicates the construction of different conceptions of promised lands and ultimately reaffirms Rachel’s choice of India as her own Promised Land—though this is not made explicit as such. India is, however, frequently identified as her home and when she finally returns to the quirkily refurbished family flat in Calcutta, the focalized narration asserts: “Ahead of her was the best part of her life. Didn’t all the wisest mathematicians say that a circle is a perfect thing? She was coming home at last.” (MMH285) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Yet Calcutta is not inured against change; its synagogues, bereft of their communities, are locked up (MMH217) and “much of the European flavour that had characterized it had dwindled since the late fifties.” (MMH224) Rachel, too, has changed in America. Her cheerfulness and joy of life is gone: “being so far from home had taken a toll on her.” (MMH237) It is her final return to India which restores her not only to her home but also to herself. It is, intriguingly, an attachment which her mother Sarah cannot share. To her the return to India feels like “moving backward, rather than forward, in her life.” (MMH282) Both, it appears, have different expectations which may be generationally and experientially determined. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The image of natural destruction with which Mahadevan concludes her <em>Shulamith</em> is also employed by Silliman in <em>The Man with Many Hats</em>, but it is revaluated. On vacation in Gopalpur, Rachel notices that the Anchorage, a building she remembers from her childhood, has been taken away by the tide, “brick by brick, just as the treacherous ebb and flow of her parents’ tempestuous marriage had eroded their family.” (MMH178) Yet for her, this does not spell resignation but new beginnings and new opportunities:
</p>

> Since that time Rachel had travelled vast distances, in mind, spirit and body. She dived eagerly into life and been caught in swirling and treacherous currents that took her in directions she had never sought to take. She found herself always learning anew, making new friends, and finding other ways of being in the world.” (MMH178–79)
