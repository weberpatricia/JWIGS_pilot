<p style="text-align: justify;">
The scholarly terrain of Indian Jewish life has been mapped mainly by anthropologists and historians (Isenberg, 1988; Katz, 2000; Roland, 1989; Weil, 2002). Their work traces how these communities arrived on the subcontinent and put down roots there. It follows how they kept the rhythms of ritual and observance while being drawn into the linguistic, social and cultural worlds around them. This work is valuable and has helped us understand these communities. However, it mostly treats Indian Jews as a subject of social or historical study. Yet comparatively little of this scholarship approaches Indian Jewish writing as literature. Novels, poems, memoirs and plays by Indian Jewish authors are often read for what they document, as ethnographic evidence or historical testimony about communal life, rather than as aesthetic works that call for attention to form, style and imaginative design. What gets lost in such readings is how these texts tell the story of identity: the narrative strategies, generic choices and figurative language through which belonging is imagined, contested and remade. As a result, Indian Jewish literary production has seldom been treated as a body of literature in its own right, one that deserves sustained critical and theoretical engagement.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
This gap matters all the more because the understanding of Jewish literature is itself changing. Dan Miron challenges the long-standing idea that Jewish literature is a single, unbroken tradition handed down through history (Miron, 2010). Instead, he suggests that we think of Jewish literatures as "contiguous." By this he means that they sit next to one another, touch at certain points, and share some concerns, without having to belong to one fixed line or centre. This idea opens Jewish writing outwards. It allows us to see Jewish literature as a network that spreads across many languages, places and cultures, which makes it very close to the idea of world literature.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Even so, when scholars talk about contemporary Jewish literature in Europe and beyond, they mostly mean writing from the Ashkenazi world. These experiences are important, but they are not the only Jewish experiences. When the Ashkenazi framework is treated as the standard, the writing of other Jewish communities, such as those in India, either becomes invisible or looks like an exception. Indian Jewish writers did not experience the same history of persecution in their home country. Their stories are shaped by other things: colonialism, the Partition, migration to Israel and the West, life as a very small minority in a large and diverse society, and long contact with Hindu, Muslim and Christian neighbours. These experiences cannot be fully understood through a framework built mainly on European Jewish history.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
This is why comparative literature is central to my research. David Damrosch understands world literature not as a fixed canon of great books but as a mode of circulation and of reading (Damrosch, 2003). In his account, texts from different places are brought into conversation with one another, and attention falls on how they move, travel and change meaning as they cross cultural and linguistic borders. Seen this way, comparative literature and world literature are closely connected. Both bring Miron's contiguities into view and show how works from separate traditions can shed light on one another. A comparative approach therefore lets Indian Jewish writing be read alongside other Jewish literatures and other Indian literatures at the same time, without forcing it into a single category.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
My thesis examines how Jewish identities negotiate their place in India. It looks at how these communities settled, adapted and endured as a small minority in a land with its own deep traditions, many languages and established social hierarchies. Their history in India is not simply one of preservation or assimilation. It is a continual negotiation between remembering where one comes from and belonging to where one is, between holding on to a distinct religious and cultural inheritance and taking part in the life of the surrounding society. At the heart of this inquiry is the body. Identity is not only an idea, a belief or a set of inherited practices. It is also lived and carried in the flesh: in how people eat and fast, dress and adorn themselves, love and marry, mourn and remember, suffer and heal. The body is where history leaves its marks, and where memory is held, sometimes more stubbornly than in words. To read identity through the body is to ask how belonging and exclusion are felt as well as thought.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
My central question, then, is this: how do Jewish bodies, marked by histories of displacement, exile and loss, meet and negotiate with other bodies in India that have endured comparable forms of corporeal trauma? To address it, I place Jewish identities in India in dialogue with India's subaltern identities, particularly Dalit identities. The two histories are very different. Jewish experiences of diaspora and Dalit experiences of caste oppression come from distinct structures of power, and neither can be reduced to the other. Read side by side, however, they show how the body can become a site of stigma, injury and exclusion, and also a site of memory, endurance and resistance. The thesis will examine how each group experiences trauma through the body, how that trauma is remembered and passed on across generations, and how individuals and communities respond to it: through silence, testimony, ritual, defiance or creative reinvention. I will pursue these questions through close comparative readings of four primary novels, paying attention not only to what they say about corporeal trauma but to how they represent it. That means looking at their narrative form, their imagery and language, and the imaginative strategies through which they give suffering and survival a shape on the page.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
This project tries to offer that kind of coherent thinking, even if not on the grand scale Miron imagined. It uses an interdisciplinary and comparative approach, bringing together literary studies, postcolonial studies, memory studies and studies of the body. Through this, it tries to understand the contiguities of Indian Jewish literature: the points where it touches, overlaps with and speaks to other literatures. I read Indian Jewish literature as a tributary of the wider Jewish literary complex, a smaller stream that flows into the larger river while keeping its own character, shaped by the different settings of postcolonial India.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The primary corpus is four semi-autobiographical novels by Indian women writers whose work engages with Jewish or Dalit positionalities. I chose women writers deliberately, for two reasons. First, these texts pay close attention to the gendered side of minority identity: being a woman within a minority community brings its own pressures, expectations and forms of violence. Second, they show how memory and the body are lived and negotiated through female experience. Women in these novels often carry family memory, cultural practice and the marks of violence in their own bodies. By placing Jewish and Dalit women's writing side by side, the project aims to open a multidirectional dialogue between subaltern identities that are rarely studied together. I take the idea of multidirectional memory from Michael Rothberg, who argues that memories of different histories of suffering do not compete with each other for attention. Instead, they can borrow from, echo and illuminate one another (Rothberg, 2009). Following Rothberg, this reading does not rank one suffering above another. It asks what each experience can teach us about the other.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Baumgartner's Bombay (1988) by Anita Desai explores the diasporic entanglements of Jewish identity in India. The novel follows Hugo Baumgartner, a German Jew who escapes Nazi Germany and spends the rest of his life in India, where he never fully belongs. Desai, who has German heritage on her mother's side, builds the narrative around archival traces and letters, and weaves personal history together with the larger traumas of the twentieth century. Baumgartner's body is marked by his Jewishness and by exile. He is seen as foreign in Germany and foreign again in India. His body becomes a powerful image of survival, alienation and the persistence of memory: what lives on in a person even after home has been lost. The Book of Esther (2002) by Esther David is an openly Jewish Indian narrative about Jewish life in India, especially in the Bene Israel community. It interlaces personal and family memory with everyday practices such as food and recipes, documenting how Jewish identity is kept alive and negotiated within the larger Indian cultural world. The novel shows that Jewish identity can survive and continue outside the West. It also shows how women's experience and the memory held in the body, in cooking, ritual and the passing down of customs, help keep religion and culture alive across generations. The God of Small Things (1997) by Arundhati Roy is not written by a Dalit author, but it gives important insight into caste oppression. It draws on the dynamics of the writer's own family in Kerala and tells a story of forbidden love. At its centre is the relationship between Ammu, an upper-caste Syrian Christian woman, and Velutha, a Dalit man. The novel shows how society watches, controls and punishes bodies that cross caste lines. The body marked by caste difference becomes a forbidden body, a place where love, desire and violence come together. Velutha's fate shows how brutally caste hierarchy defends its boundaries through the body. The Gypsy Goddess (2014) by Meena Kandasamy brings Dalit identity into sharp focus through its depiction of caste-based atrocities. It is based on the 1968 massacre of Dalit agricultural workers at Kilvenmani in Tamil Nadu. The novel bears witness to systemic oppression and shows that the body is the final target of caste violence: Dalit bodies are exploited, attacked and destroyed. Kandasamy's work matters doubly. As one of the few women writing about Dalit experience in English, she brings that experience into global literary circles. She also resists its erasure by writing from a perspective that is clearly female and grounded in the body.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The body, therefore, can be read as an archive. Conventionally, the archive is understood as an institutional space, such as a library, a museum or a state record office, where documents, correspondence and records are preserved as the authoritative material of the past. The archive is never a neutral repository. It is bound up with power from the outset, and control over the archive means control over memory itself (Derrida, 1996). Such archives decide what counts as history and whose lives are worth preserving. For minority and subaltern communities, and for the women within them in particular, the official archive is often marked by silence, absence and incompleteness. Where the official archive falls silent, the body bears its own testimony. Drawing on these insights, this thesis reads the bodies represented in Indian Jewish and Dalit women's writing as archives in their own right. In habit, scar, hunger, ritual and inherited custom, they preserve histories of displacement, exclusion and survival that the written record has forgotten.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
History in these texts is felt viscerally: in the gut and on the skin, in hunger and pain, in fear and desire, and in the involuntary returns of memory. Trauma leaves its marks on the body, and so, crucially, do survival and resistance. Some of these marks are overt and physical, like the wounds inflicted by caste violence and antisemitic persecution. Others are quieter and harder to see, working through everyday embodied experience. They include the hesitant way a displaced person moves through an unfamiliar city, practices of cooking and eating passed down from mother to daughter, the fear of touch across caste lines, and the lasting, bodily sense of never fully belonging. If the body is an archive, then reading the body becomes a way of reading history. Attending to the intersection of corporeal trauma and collective memory reveals identities that are neither fixed nor singular but hybrid (Bhabha, 1994). They take shape in the encounter between inherited histories of suffering and the lived conditions of the present, between memories of elsewhere and the demands of belonging here. The body is where these layers meet, overlap and are continually renegotiated. Reading the body in this way also lets me bring Jewish and Dalit experiences into critical conversation without collapsing their differences. In both, the body is the site where history is stored, felt and handed down across generations. This approach challenges any view of the body as merely a passive victim of history.  
</p>
