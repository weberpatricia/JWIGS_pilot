<p style="text-align: justify;">
They say human life began with a fish. So does this story. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
A recipe. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Begin with fish. A pomfret, perhaps, or whatever the boats brought in that morning, split and cleaned, rubbed with turmeric and salt, then dredged in red chilli until it looks as though it has been dipped in the colour of sunset. It goes into hot oil just before the Sabbath begins. The kitchen fills with smoke and spice, and somewhere beyond the open door the Arabian Sea goes on doing what it has done for two thousand years: arriving, and arriving, and arriving.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
This is how Esther David's Book of Rachel (2006) opens its doors to us, not with a character but with a recipe. Rachel Dandekar is the last Jew in Danda, a fishing village on the Konkan coast. Her husband Aaron is dead. Her children have gone to Israel, and so have her neighbours, one family after another, until the Bene Israel houses along the lane have fallen silent. What she still has is a small synagogue she sweeps every day, a sea her ancestors are said to have come out of, and a kitchen where the Sabbath is still kept, even when there is no one at the table to keep it with her. When developers come for the synagogue and the land beneath it, this quiet widow becomes, to everyone's surprise, its fiercest defender. Every chapter of the novel begins with a recipe. The titles read like a page torn from a mother's notebook: "Fried Fish," "Mutton Curry with Tamarind," "Bombay Duck," "Tandlya chi Bhakhri," "Malida." I want to argue that these recipes are not garnish. They are the novel's spine and its archive. In Book of Rachel, food holds three kinds of memory at once: the mythic memory of origins on the Danda shore, the intimate memory of a woman's kitchen and her absent children, and the braided memory of a Jewish people who have lived so long inside Marathi culture that its spices have become their own. The novel is soaked in nostalgia. But it is a nostalgia that cooks, and in the battle over the synagogue it learns to fight.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>
•	 What the Sea Gave Back</strong>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The Bene Israel tell their beginning as a shipwreck. Long ago, the story goes, a ship carrying Jews fleeing persecution broke apart off this coast. Seven men and seven women were washed ashore alive. They buried their dead in the sand, lost their scriptures to the water, and kept only what could be carried in the body: the Sabbath, the dietary laws, and one line of prayer, the Shema. They became oil-pressers. Because they would not work on Saturdays, their neighbours named them Shanivar Telis, the Saturday oil-men. A people remade itself out of salt water and memory. Rachel lives inside that story without needing to tell it. Danda's beach is not scenery in the novel; it is the threshold where everything began, and the waves that break on it are the same waves that once broke the ship. When she fries the Sabbath fish, she is cooking with the coast's own gifts: the catch, the coconut, the sour purple kokum, the rice from the paddy. Each Friday she repeats, without ceremony, the community's first act of survival: taking what this shore offered and folding it into Jewish time. The kitchen is where the shipwreck is survived again, every week. Rachel's Danda is a milieu. She needs no plaque to remember the drowned ancestors. She has the sea at her door and the dishes it feeds her. That is precisely what makes the developers' offer so violent, though no one in the novel raises a hand. To sell the synagogue and its land is to sever the thread that runs from the wreck to the shore to the Sabbath plate, and to turn a living memory into a story that someone, somewhere else, might one day remember having heard.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>
•	A Place Set for the Absent</strong>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
If the sea keeps the community's oldest memory, the kitchen keeps its tenderest, and the keepers are women. Rachel knows things no book has taught her. She knows when the rice must be soaked and how long, how to bake kanavali, the semolina cake rich with coconut milk, cardamom and raisins, a day ahead so that no fire is lit on the Sabbath. She knows that the vessel for tea is kept apart from the others, as the law requires. This is knowledge held in the wrists and the nose, passed from mother to daughter by watching rather than by reading.  The recipes that open each chapter take this knowledge out of the body and set it down in ink. A recipe is a strange and hopeful kind of writing. It speaks in the imperative, soak, grind, temper, stir, to a cook who has not yet arrived, and it assumes a future in which the dish will be made again. Read this way, the whole novel becomes the notebook a mother fills for children who have gone away: here is how to make the fish, here is how to keep the festival, here is how to remain yourselves in a country that does not smell of kokum. And food is where Rachel's loneliness lives. Certain dishes belong to certain people, so that a single bitter green, cooked on an ordinary afternoon, can bring back the son who loved it as surely as a photograph. To cook it in his absence is to lay a place at the table for someone who will not come. Rachel's stove keeps that promise warm. When her daughter Zephra joins her in the fight for the synagogue, it feels, briefly, as though the promise has been kept.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>
•	Coconut Milk Instead of Cream</strong>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Taste Rachel's food and you cannot tell where the Jew ends and the Konkani begins. That is the point. Sol kadhi, the pink coconut curry soured with kokum and thickened with rice paste, is a coastal Maharashtrian dish. Fish alberas, green with herbs and coconut milk, comes from the same kitchens. Yet both are cooked to a Jewish clock and under Jewish law. Meat never meets milk, so the coconut stands in where another diaspora would reach for cream. Only fish with fins and scales go into the pot. The Sabbath food is ready before the first star. Even Passover goes by a Marathi name in the novel: "Anashi Dhakacha San." Nowhere is this braiding more beautiful than in Malida, the rite that names one of the chapters. Malida is a Bene Israel thanksgiving offered in the name of the prophet Elijah, Eliyahu Hanabi. A platter of flattened rice is mixed with grated coconut, sugar and fruit, then blessed and shared. No other Jewish community has anything like it, and nothing in it could have grown anywhere but here. It is a prayer you can only make with Indian hands. Rachel's devotions carry the same double accent: she hums a Marathi "The Lord is My Shepherd" as she mops the synagogue floor, and sings a bhajan to the baby Moses floating on the Nile, adapted from a Marathi bhajan to the child Krishna. This is not a merely culinary observation; it is the novel's politics. The developers, and the committee eager to sell, see a remnant waiting for its ticket out. Rachel's kitchen shows something else: a people whose belonging is written into the very taste of the food. The Bene Israel did not simply live in Danda. Danda lives in them.
</p>

<p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>
•	The Lamp in the Empty Room</strong>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Book of Rachel is written against a real emptying. After 1948 most of India's Bene Israel left for Israel, and villages like Danda, once full of Sabbath lamps, went dark one window at a time. Esther David has put that loss in a single sentence: when a community decreases in number, its traditional food becomes a memory. The novel lives inside that sentence. Rachel cooks festival dishes for a household of one. She polishes the synagogue for a congregation that no longer comes. She lights the lamp on Friday evening in a room where no one else will see it burn. Her kitchen and her synagogue mirror each other: two rooms where ritual goes on after the people it was meant for have gone. Restorative nostalgia wants to rebuild the lost home brick by brick, exactly as it was. Reflective nostalgia lingers in the ache itself, and accepts that the past will not return whole. Rachel's longing is mostly the second kind. She knows her children will not come back to live in Danda, and she does not cook to stop the clock. But the threat to the synagogue sharpens her grief into something with a spine. She will not let memory become a private thing, kept in a pot on one woman's stove. The synagogue is where the community's memory has an address, four walls and a door. Without it, the Bene Israel of Danda would become a recipe with no one left to eat it. Joined by Judah, a lawyer and her son's friend, and by Zephra, Rachel takes on the developers and the town's assumption that her people's story is already finished. It matters that she is no leader, no scholar, no rabbi. David suggests that the kitchen's kind of memory, patient, repetitive and stubbornly attached to place, is exactly the kind that can hold a line. To keep cooking the old dishes and to refuse to give up the synagogue are, for Rachel, the same act: a way of staying. Nostalgia, so often dismissed as sentimental and soft, turns out in her hands to be a form of resistance.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;"><strong>
•	A Recipe Is a Promise</strong>
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
In Book of Rachel, Esther David asks a recipe to carry what elsewhere might be carried by a history book or a monument. The recipes at the head of each chapter tie every episode to the shore of the shipwreck, to the knowledge women pass from hand to hand, and to a culture in which Jewish law and Konkani produce are cooked together until they cannot be told apart. Rachel's cooking is full of longing, but it is not an escape. It keeps her company in her solitude, keeps her children present at a table they have left, and in the end gives her the strength to defend the synagogue.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The novel's last gift is in its form. Because David sets recipe beside story, she leaves the reader holding instructions as well as memories. Anyone can soak the rice, grind the coconut, sour the curry with kokum and make sown kadhi, or bake kanavali on a Friday afternoon. In doing so, however far from Danda, the reader takes a small part in the remembering the novel exists to do. Years later David would make this project explicit in her cookbook Bene Appétit: The Cuisine of Indian Jews (2021). But Book of Rachel had already shown what that book would argue. For a people who are disappearing, food is not only something to be remembered. It is how remembering is done. So, in the end, as the novel begins, with fish. The oil is hot. The chilli is bright. The sea is still arriving. Somewhere a lamp is lit for the Sabbath, and someone is setting the table for people to devour the food and get immersed in stories. 
</p>
