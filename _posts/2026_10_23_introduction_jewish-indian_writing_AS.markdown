<p style="text-align: justify;">
The corpus of Indian Jewish writing is small, and studies about Jewish communities in India have traditionally been based on anthropological rather than literary approaches. They have focused in particular on the different experiences of the three main communities of Indian Jews—Bene Israel, Baghdadis, and Cochin Jews—as well as the more recently recognized communities of the Bnai Ephraim and Bnai Menashe. Crucial also for a literary inquiry, these communities defy established categories of Jewishness, being distinct from the socio-political categories of Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Mizrahi; nor is the binary of religious and secular generally a useful descriptor in the Indian context (Guttman 2023: 75), though some of the more recent texts clearly envisage the alternative of a secular and atheistic existence in India (Stähler 2026 forthcoming). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Having been exposed to different external influences in India, the “Jewishness” of these communities, their identities, and their negotiation of the colonial and postcolonial periods in India are strongly heterogeneous (Roland 1998; Weil 2002a; Silliman 2016). The presumably mythical origin of the Bene Israel, the largest Jewish community in India, dates back to antiquity. Mostly living in rural Maharashtra, they increasingly shaped an Indian identity for themselves (Roland 2019: 23), though they retained customs that are considered Jewish (Weil 2002b: 80). In the mid-nineteenth century, their being “discovered” initiated the education of the previously isolated Bene Israel in traditional Judaism and their migration to urban centers, mostly Bombay (Mumbai) and Poona (Pune) in Maharashtra, but also Ahmedabad in Gujarat, and other cities in India. Under British colonial rule, some Bene Israel rose to prominence, gaining positions within the colonial government and the Indian Army (Weil 2002a: 26).
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The more recently arrived Baghdadis questioned the Jewishness of the Bene Israel and considered them (too) “Indian.” Settled in India in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, initially in Bombay and then mostly shifting to Calcutta (Kolkata), the economic and administrative center of the Raj, these Jews from Arab countries experienced the transculturation from a Judeo-Arabic identity to a Judeo-British identity, in which their Jewish identity remained constant and primary (Silliman 2001: 48, 50). Even more than the Bene Israel, the Baghdadis “aligned with British rule” (Silliman 2016: 71) and continually made efforts “to establish their status as European” during the colonial period (Roland 2019: 23). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The Cochin Jews in the southern Indian state of Kerala, considered the oldest Jewish community in India, are also referred to as the “black” Jews of Cochin. Their community was enriched with Sephardic “white” Jews (Paradesi) following the Jewish expulsion from Spain in 1492, yet the two communities maintained their ethnic and cultural differences (Weil 2009: 322).
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The challenge for Indian Jewish communities lay predominantly in preserving their traditions while adapting to new religious, national, and linguistic contexts, thus creating a dynamic interplay between tradition and modernity, that was framed within the broader context of colonial and postcolonial identity formation. The competing Jewish identities of Bene Israel and Baghdadis impacted the attitudes both communities developed toward nationalist and communal issues in the transitional period between colonial rule and independence as well as after. Partition and independence forced especially Baghdadi Jews, previously largely aligned with the colonial elite, to define themselves beyond their Jewishness and led to processes of cultural hybridization (Silliman 2001: 20). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Jewish identities were further complicated in post-independence India after the emergence of the state of Israel. Confronted with the traumatic experience of Partition and shifting power relations following independence, which reconfigured Jewish positionalities, many Indian Jews chose to emigrate. Bene Israel mostly went to the new Jewish State (Isenberg 1995: 22), but only few Baghdadis emigrated to Israel; they predominantly chose destinations in the anglophone world (Roland 2019: 30). The majority of Cochin Jews emigrated initially to Israel and, since the late twentieth century, also the US (Johnson 1995: 33–5). Confronted with dominant Israeli constructions of Jewishness, emigration to Israel entailed problems of absorption experienced by the Bene Israel and Cochin Jews in the Promised Land (Isenberg 1995: 22) as well as the increasing loss of their communal identity for the few Baghdadi Jews who emigrated to Israel (Roland 1995: 44). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Altogether amounting to a minuscule percentage of Indian society and continually dwindling because of emigration, the three main Jewish communities have produced in India and in various post-migratory contexts since the early 1960s a distinct, if diminutive, corpus of Indian Jewish writing—mostly in English, rather than any of the Indian languages—which is largely based on autobiographical, if frequently fictionalized, writing. Their cultural, historical, and socio-political situatedness at the margins of the Global South and in various post-migratory settings makes the writing of Indian Jews a highly valuable and original case study with regard to the intricacies of the Jewish literary complex.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Indian Jewish literary production is firmly anchored not only in the negotiation of Jewish identities, but of Jewish identities in the context of the historical, cultural, and socio-political developments impacting the subcontinent—including the arrival of European Jewish exiles during the Second World War as well as the creation of Israel—and determining cultural and socio-political interactions. Common to all three major Indian Jewish communities is that their literary production since the early 1960s, arising mainly from among the Bene Israel and Baghdadis, has been “dominated by women” (Guttman 2023: 72). Writers of note include Esther David (India), Sophie Judah (Israel), Ruby Daniels (Israel), and Carmit Delman (America).
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Individual research articles about Indian Jewish literature, in particular by Yulia Egorova (2003), Pallavi Rastogi (2013), and Anna M. Guttman (2023) have laid important but scant and scattered groundwork for an approach to Indian Jewish writing. Its cultural function has been identified by Guttman as “documenting and preserving the community’s history and culture in the face of demographic decline and limited knowledge about the community among both Indians and Jews” (2023: 72). Egorova has observed about the Bene Israel writer Meera Mahadevan that she constructs “a model of cultural relativity and otherness” (2003: 78). Guttman argues that “contemporary Indian Jewish literature reframes Indian history, complicating the memory of partition and recovering a narrative of lost, Indigenous cosmopolitanism” and notes that these texts “look back on and transmit a marginalized Indian Jewish cultural identity” (2023: 72, 73); they are frequently conflicted, both internally and externally.
</p>
