<p style="text-align: justify;">
Meera Mahadevan (nee Miriam Aaron Jacob Mendrekar) was born into the Bene Israel community of Karachi in 1931. After Partition, her family fled to Bombay. Here, she met and married the Hindu writer and journalist T. K. Mahadevan (Egorova 2003: 71), who was immersed in Gandhian thought and edited the journal <em>Gandhi Marg</em>. Active in social relief work, Meera lived for some time in one of Mahatma Gandhi’s ashrams in Maharashtra, where she was not only inspired by his social ideals but where the quotidian use of spoken Hindi was promoted and may have helped her to become proficient in the language (Wessler 2013: 199). Her pioneering novel <em>Apna Ghar</em> (My Home) was published in Hindi in 1961 and reissued in 1973; it appeared in a free translation into English by the author with the title <em>Shulamith</em> in 1975. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Saturated with autobiographical detail, <em>Shulamith</em> unfolds the story of a Bene Israel family in Bombay. When her husband, Michael, decides to emigrate to Israel, the eponymous main character refuses to accompany him. She remains in Bombay, where the family migrated after Partition from Karachi, to care for their adolescent son Uriel, Michael’s much younger sister Mezuzah (known as Maizie), and her mother-in-law. They are eventually joined by Michael’s brother Daniel from England after the passing of his British wife. Their modestly affluent family supports impoverished Bene Israel and takes in the young Naomi who has been wrongfully rejected by her husband. Maizie, initially engaged to a member of the Bene Israel community, falls in love with a Hindu. She is forced to forego the relationship and to abort her unlooked-for pregnancy; she eventually marries Naomi’s brother Issac who, a product of his social circumstances, turns out to be violent and a criminal. After the death of Shulamith’s mother-in-law, Uriel quickly weds his girlfriend Simha, so they, too, can emigrate to Israel. Increasingly suffering from depression, Shulamith wastes away and when Michael unexpectedly returns from Israel, she lies dying.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The novel is prefaced with an epigraph collated from some later passages which contrast the allure of Israel with familial responsibility. Between these two markers, <em>Shulamith</em> negotiates three main contemporary concerns relevant to the Bene Israel in India: Conflicts arising from the positionality of Indian Jewish identity between India and Israel; the survival of the community in the face of emigration and modernization as well as, more specifically, its injunctions on marrying out; and social criticism, both within the Bene Israel community and the wider Indian context. Most of these concerns recur in some form or other throughout Indian Jewish writing.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Israel is presented in the novel as a promising yet problematic entity which ultimately upsets the precious balance of Jewish life in India. While its existence is never challenged, its ethical foundations are subjected to intense and highly critical scrutiny in the novel. The perceived disruptive impact of the new Jewish State is foreshadowed not only in its epigraph but dominates the narrative from the very beginning when Shulamith sees her husband off with “a heavy heart” (S7) as he leaves for Israel. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Michael feels compelled to go to Israel for religious reasons. Yet once in the Promised Land, he becomes intensely aware of his Indian identity. Comparing his own to the origins of American and Baghdadi Jews in Israel, he denies the latter the same degree of Indianness: “I may be Jewish but I cannot think or live like an American or Baghdadi Jew. I am Indian, and my culture is an Indian culture. India is my country.” (S203) From his perspective, the Baghdadis’ anglicized otherness seems to admit an existence outside India more easily. Mahadevan, confronting and turning around pre-existing Baghdadi stereotypes about the Indianness of the Bene Israel, thus implicitly construes them as more Indian than the cosmopolitan Baghdadis. By having Michael emigrate to Israel and then return to India, Mahadevan attributes to him at the very end of the novel the role of an arbiter whose experience comprises life in both of these competing spaces of Jewish existence and confirms Shulamith’s apprehensions. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Written in the aftermath of the catastrophe of European Jewry, Mahadevan’s novel contrasts the experience of the Bene Israel in stark words to that of the Ashkenazim. She insists that India is the only country which is not blighted by antisemitism: “the peace that we have had here no other Jewish community could enjoy elsewhere. No one ever looked down upon our religion here, nor did they have contempt for our customs.” (S40) The trope, based on historical experience, is variously reiterated in Indian Jewish writing.[1]  
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The absence of antisemitism in India is contrasted by Shulamith directly to the Holocaust in Mahadevan’s novel; it ultimately affirms a Jewish exceptionalism in India. Initially, Shulamith suggests through her use of the personal pronoun “us” an identification with those Jews who were the victims of the Holocaust: “Remember what they did to us in Germany?” (S40) But this identification is revoked when the need of Israel as a shelter is emphasized for those victimized Jews—in contrast to the Indian Jews who allegedly have never experienced any persecution and therefore do not rely on Israel for their survival; the Jewish past in India is consequently evoked as the guarantor also of the Jewish future in India with the understanding that the Jews will never be persecuted there (S40–1). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The absolute validity of Indian Jewish exceptionalism is nevertheless questioned in the novel with the traumatic experience of Partition, though this is clearly identified as a violent upheaval which affects all of Indian society. Reflecting the writer’s own experience of exile, it emerges that Shulamith’s family had to flee from Karachi and found refuge with the Bene Israel community in Bombay. The violence done to them during the turmoil of Partition is graphically, if briefly, described with the looting of the synagogue and the desecration of the holy books. The point is strongly made, however, that the local Muslim community “deeply regretted what had happened” (S57); Mahadevan’s conciliatory impulse is fervently articulated when the family’s Muslim neighbor in Karachi insists that “the true Muslim will always continue to be a brother to a Bene-Israel. It is not possible to wipe out this blood relation.” (S58) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The point made here is one that is of utmost significance to Mahadevan also in relation to the uncertainties resulting from the creation of Israel. While Shulamith reflects on the motives of the perpetrators, she muses—ahistorically—if they were “urged to it by the Arab-Israeli conflict?” (S57) The potentially negative impact of Israel on Indian Jewish exceptionalism in this context, beyond the ambivalent attraction of making Aliyah, is also evoked in later Indian Jewish texts and anticipates contemporary anxieties rampant in the wake of the massacre of October 7, 2023 and the subsequent Gaza War. Yet it is emphatically questioned by Mahadevan with the insistence on the distinct Indian Jewish identity of the Bene Israel: “But we are Indians. We have been here for so long, and we have never done anyone any harm.” (S57) 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The memory of the trauma of Partition is complemented with a private memory from Shulamith’s childhood when a fellow pupil at her Christian Mission School accused the Jews of being Christ killers (S55). The accusation, of Christian provenance and a transplant from Europe to the Indian context, is answered nonchalantly by Shulamith with the remark that Jesus was Jewish himself. The episode nevertheless results in a reprimand, and her mother henceforth sends her to another school. Both, the trauma of Partition and religious intolerance appear to contradict Shulamith’s absolute trust in India. Yet both are, significantly, products of European colonialist intervention in India; they, just like the Arab–Israeli conflict, are external influences, which Mahadevan seems to cite in confirmation of the Indian Jewish exceptionalism she claims.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Mahadevan nevertheless does not shy away from emphasizing internal incompatibilities within the Bene Israel community. Shulamith’s and her community’s uprooting from Karachi initiates a difficult process of integration in Bombay which, the novel suggests, is to some extent a generational issue. The parallel to the Bene Israel transplant to Israel is never made explicit by Mahadevan, though Michael’s inability to find a new home in the Promised Land and subsequent return to India in contrast to Uriel’s permanent emigration suggest a similar pattern.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
As indicated above, the novel offers a comprehensive exploration of the negotiation of Indian Jewish identities in the force field between India and Israel. Uriel’s motivation for leaving for Israel, in contrast to his father’s, is not religious but ideological. As such, it reflects another generational shift which Mahadevan perceptively signals in her novel. Uriel—like the impoverished Issac, who is drawn to Israel not because of his Jewishness but for the “hope” it offers to the poor (S47)—is struck with the beauty of the “pure socialist state” (S91). Sidelining religion, he in fact emphasizes that the Israelis are neither “fanatics,” nor “even overly orthodox.” (S91) Uriel’s perception very much reflects the early years of the Jewish State before demographic and political shifts in the wake of the “miraculous” victory in the Six-Day War, which initiated a continuous rise of nationalist-orthodox forces in Israel (Kaplan 2025: 119–20). 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Shulamith, in contrast, challenges the righteousness of Israel’s actions by insisting that “the birth of Israel has spelt misery to some thousands of people. It has robbed them of their home. Thousands of Arabs have been forced into becoming refugees. I feel that it is not right.” (S92) The solution to the “Arab problem” is crucial to her, and it requires the recognition that “they are after all our brothers, only then shall we be able to build our nation into a great nation.” (S92) This soul-searching commentary from beyond the Ashkenazi-centric perspective articulates concerns which have a bearing not only on the State of Israel but on all Jews and suggests a programmatic ethical imperative.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Mahadevan’s novel comprises three generations. Ma is the matriarch of the family. Her Jewish observance is uncompromised. Of her three sons, Gabriel fell in the Second World War; Daniel went to Britain and married an Englishwoman; Michael, leaving his family, sought to emigrate to Israel. Both of Ma’s surviving sons are construed by Mahadevan as paradigms of predominant trajectories within the Bene Israel community which challenge their Jewishness and their Indianness, respectively. They exemplify emigration to England on the one hand, or Aliyah to Israel on the other. Yet in confirmation of Indian Jewish exceptionalism, both Daniel and Michael ultimately return to India, though they have been changed by their experiences abroad. Daniel has been anglicized and modernized and offers a perspective on social progress; Michael, returned from Israel and having become “quite bold” (S201), affirms the Indian component of his identity.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Daniel’s tolerant and modernizing tendencies are explained by his being exposed to western society in Britain. Toward the socially inferior Naomi, whom he will later marry, he insists on the primacy of “basic humanity” (S28) over societal norms and conventions. His determination to level culturally constructed differences and to challenge asymmetrical power relations by invoking a common humanity reflects the Gandhian values which have been attributed to Mahadevan. Her novel elaborates a clear moral imperative calculated to improve “the fate of our poor people.” (S168) Significantly, the recognition of this responsibility refers to the Indian people rather than merely to the Bene Israel and projects the social involvement of the minority within majority culture (S168). It is this moral imperative which determines Maizie to become a nurse and contribute actively to the social improvement of India.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
The ills of religious intransigence are sketched in the novel in two examples which confront the common humanity invoked by Daniel with the need of the Bene Israel community for self-preservation. Shulamith’s brother is prevented by their mother from the cross-community marriage to a “Brahmin girl” because she is afraid that her grandchildren might be “branded as black Bene-Israelis” (S85). The community of the Bene Israel was split into <em>gora</em> (the Marathi word for white) and <em>kala</em> (black) Israel; contact between both groups was restricted because the <em>kala</em>, offspring of interethnic or interreligious encounters, were considered defiling (Isenberg 1988: 104–07).[2] In Mahadevan’s novel, the continued segregation between <em>gora</em> and <em>kala</em> is explained with the perpetuation of the community and its perceived purity: 
</p>

> We have no other alternative. We still keep such children away from our families. Otherwise how do you think we could survive in this country? Religion is old-fashioned. You cannot modernise it: you cannot compromise with it! (S85)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
The enforced endogamy encapsulates the dilemma of the dwindling Bene Israel community in India between integration and emigration. Marrying out is, next to the allure of Israel, the second conundrum for the community; it is also the second central concern of the novel.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Maizie, only a little older than Shulamith’s son Uriel with whom she grows up like sister and brother is, in fact, Shulamith’s sister-in-law. She is initially a carefree and happy young woman who moves in the higher circles of society and is engaged to the scion of one of the established Bene Israel families in Bombay. Yet she falls in love with a Hindu who, with the one exception of not being Jewish, is fully the other’s equal. And yet, he can never be acceptable as a husband to a Bene Israel. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
When Maizie falls pregnant, their illicit love affair is forcefully terminated by Shulamith and her pregnancy is aborted. It takes Maizie a long time to emerge from the resulting near catatonic state. To protect her, and her family’s status in the Bene Israel community, she is eventually married off far below her own social standing to Issac. 
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
Through the characters of Issac and his sister Naomi, Mahadevan opens a perspective on the socially deprived fringes of the Bene Israel community which intertwine with the underbelly of Indian majority society. Rescued by Uriel from her abusive marriage to Issac, Maizie decides to train to become a nurse. As she is congratulated by a doctor at the hospital on her excellent grades, Maizie is surprised to recognize in her another Bene Israel, though this is initially obscured by her Hindu surname. The markers of her belonging are mainly derived from essentialist premises and comprise phenomenological features (face) as well as cultural practices (intonation) (S179). The doctor is, like Mahadevan herself, another Bene Israel who married out and has “gone through similar anguish herself.” (S180) Otherwise without any function in the narrative, she explains to Maizie, presumably articulating the author’s own view: 
</p>

> You find a husband of your choice outside the community but for this you must renounce your family, your religion and the whole tradition within which you have grown up. For a while you may not miss these things; your immediate happiness compensates, but gradually you have an urge to return. But how? Would you be welcomed? That is the question. No one calls. They don’t wish to see you. Your name is mercilessly removed from the list of family members. And even if you could return, one would have changed, oneself. There would be strain, tension, a lack of communication. This is the sum of your punishment. (S181)

<p style="text-align: justify;">
The sympathetic doctor derives from her experience, which she frames as an implicit indictment, another social imperative which is directed specifically at the Bene Israel community: “If my daughter were to marry a husband of her own choosing I would never make her suffer the misery I have gone through. I would never deprive her of my love…” (S181)
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
While there is some hope for Maizie, and while the return of both Daniel and Michael to India seems to suggest a trajectory toward a modernized and more humane Bene Israel community and for a better India, the novel ends rather bleakly. Shulamith, weakened by the constant renegotiation of her and her family’s position, dies a symbolic death. In the novel’s last paragraph, Michael wistfully remembers a sandcastle he and Shulamith built by the sea as he was still courting her. It was carried away by the strong wind, and his memories leave him now with a pervading sense of the foundations of their existence similarly sinking into the ground. (S208) The novel’s ending appears to reflect the uncertainties experienced by Indian Jews in the first decades after Indian independence not only in the face of challenges to their community cohesion but also with regard to their stagnant entrenchment.
</p>
